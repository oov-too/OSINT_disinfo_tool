# Narrative seed of the pilot pack (phase 6b: one narrative page, attacked)

The narrative method (tools/narratives.md v0.4: pack, build, attack, revise, fork, synchrony, register; the five axes; no composite score) is core. The pilot seeds one thesis and names its rival; the phase 6b run builds one page from the ten intakes and attacks it with the core attack tests.

## The thesis: managed knowledge (key `managed-knowledge`)

From 2010 at the latest, people within the Post Office and Fujitsu recorded in writing that Horizon had bugs that affected branch accounts and that Fujitsu could alter branch data, and the Post Office went on prosecuting, pursuing shortfalls and telling the public, Parliament and the courts that Horizon was robust and that remote alteration was impossible; that knowledge was managed out of disclosure and out of the public record (through the handling of its expert's evidence, its internal working groups and its response to its critics) until the group litigation forced it out in 2019.

What it rests on in the held sources (the page will cite rows, not these lines): the RPM notes of 29 September 2010 ("Potential impact upon ongoing legal cases"); the Clarke advice of 15 July 2013 (the expert "failed to disclose material known to him"); Second Sight's interim report (8 July 2013); the 2015 statements on remote access as The Register reports them; the Common Issues judgment on the X Working Group and "a culture of secrecy" (at [42]); the Horizon Issues judgment on Balancing Transactions and APPSUP (at [315], [167]); Hamilton at [16] to [18] (remote amendment; no PEAK or KEL disclosed); Vennells's and Bates's evidence (Days 142 and 117).

## The rival: good-faith reliance (key `good-faith-reliance`)

The Post Office's executives believed Horizon was robust because their supplier, their audits and every prosecution told them so; the information about bugs and remote access sat with Fujitsu and with junior staff and did not reach the people who made the statements; the failures were of competence, structure and oversight (no in-house expertise, reliance on the supplier as adviser), not of knowledge withheld.

What it rests on in the held sources: Bates's own words to the committee that POL "very much relied upon their supplier to also be their IT experts and advisors" (The Register, 2024); Vennells on Day 142 ("At the time, they were not considered to be false"); the Clarke advice's placing of the failure on the expert and his employer; POL's Reply of 2015; Fujitsu's 2024 statements (which cut both ways: bugs admitted, POL's awareness asserted).

## Attack plan for phase 6b (the core attack tests applied to the seed)

- the walls test on both: the ordinary grounds (privilege, a pending inquiry, a police investigation, a restriction order) excluded before any closure is read as concealment;
- the first-utterance test against the catalyst list (packs/pilot/seeds/catalysts.md; threshold the Computer Weekly story of 11 May 2009);
- the divergence rule on the two tellings of Sparrow (Vennells: the scheme's project name; Bates: a covert team monitoring the scheme) and on remote access (POL 2015, POL 2019, Fujitsu 2024);
- the lens tests of packs/pilot/lenses/sfo-cooperation.json, K1 to K4 (K1 is the built-in counterexample: a lawful privilege claim is not concealment);
- independence by upstream: the press pieces and the forum relay the court and each other; count roots, not mouths.
